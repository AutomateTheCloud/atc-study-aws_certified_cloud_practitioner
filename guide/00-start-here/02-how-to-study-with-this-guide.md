# You learn by pulling ideas out of memory, spread over time, not by reading them again

Answering questions from memory, with feedback, works better than rereading, and spreading that practice over weeks makes it last. Every page in this guide is built around those two ideas.

**For:** an adult studying alone, possibly new to IT. **Time:** about 5 minutes, plus 10 to write your plan. Reading works on a phone.

By the end of this page you can explain why answering from memory beats rereading (objective 00.02a), and you have a written study plan (objective 00.02b).

## Four habits that work

- **Retrieval:** pull an idea out of your own memory: answer a question, explain a term aloud or sketch a diagram without looking. Each success makes the idea easier to reach next time, more so with **feedback** that explains why an answer is right and the others wrong.
- **Spacing:** come back to a topic after days or weeks. The effort of recalling what you half forgot is what makes it stick, which is why each lesson opens with a warm-up on earlier modules.
- **Interleaving:** once you know each topic, mix practice on topics that are easy to confuse, as the exam does when it asks you to choose between services that sound alike. The practice exams and the optional mixed sets do this.
- **Judge by results, not feelings.** Rereading builds **familiarity**, which feels like knowing, but recognizing an idea isn't producing it. Effective study often feels harder; judge a session by what you can do afterwards without the page.

Drilling the same questions until you recognize them raises your score on those questions, not your understanding. Never use "real exam questions" shared online: [The exam at a glance](03-the-exam-at-a-glance.md) explains why.

> **Your turn:** A friend says: "I read the chapter twice and it all made sense, so I know it." In two or three sentences, say what they are mistaking for knowledge, and suggest a better test.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

They are mistaking familiarity for knowledge: the ideas felt easy because the page was in front of them. A better test is to close the book and explain the main points, or answer questions, from memory. Whatever they can't produce is what to study next.

</details>

## Use each page as a loop: try, check, correct, return

Every lesson has a warm-up, a few guesses before you read, "Your turn" tasks and practice questions; each module ends with a mastery check. **Answer first, in writing or aloud, then open the feedback.** Opening it first turns retrieval back into rereading, while a wrong answer followed by feedback still teaches you. Then reread the section you missed, and meet the topic again later as a question.

## Write your study plan

People who decide in advance *when* and *where* they'll study are more likely to do it, though the effect fades after the first weeks, so review the plan as you go. Fill this in now, on paper or in a note:

| Question | Your answer |
|---|---|
| Which days and times will you study? (About 5 hours a week, in sessions of 30 to 90 minutes) | |
| Where will you study, and on what device? | |
| When is your first session, and what will you do in it? | |
| If you miss a session, when will you make it up? | |
| When will you look at this plan again? (We suggest the end of each module) | |
| Where will you keep notes on questions you got wrong? | |

**What to expect:** the standard path takes about 7 weeks at 5 hours a week, including the guardrails primer (an estimate until we test the guide with learners). You'll get warm-up questions wrong, especially at first: that's the method working. When progress feels slow, your notes on wrong answers you can now get right are your evidence.
